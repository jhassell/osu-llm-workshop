3.1 Buffer Capacity: The Limit of Resistance

Buffers don't resist pH changes forever. Buffer capacity (β) is the amount of strong acid or base a buffer can absorb before the pH changes significantly (typically by 1 unit).

What Determines Buffer Capacity?

  • Total concentration: Higher [HA] + [A⁻] = higher capacity
  • Ratio: Maximum capacity when [A⁻] = [HA] (pH = pKₐ)
  • Volume: More solution = more total moles to absorb acid/base

β ≈ 2.3 × [HA] × [A⁻] / ([HA] + [A⁻])

This simplifies to β ≈ 0.576 × Ctotal when [HA] = [A⁻] (where Ctotal = [HA] + [A⁻]).

3.2 What Happens When Capacity Is Exceeded?

Once one component is nearly consumed, the buffer can no longer neutralize added acid/base effectively. The pH then changes rapidly — the solution behaves like a weak acid or base alone.

Example: Acetate Buffer Exhaustion

1 L of 0.10 M CH₃COOH / 0.10 M CH₃COO⁻ buffer (pH 4.76). Add 0.12 mol HCl.

  • Initial: 0.10 mol HA, 0.10 mol A⁻
  • HCl reacts: H⁺ + A⁻ → HA
  • After reaction: 0.22 mol HA, –0.02 mol A⁻ (impossible!)

All A⁻ is consumed after 0.10 mol HCl. The remaining 0.02 mol H⁺ makes the solution strongly acidic (pH ≈ 1.7). The buffer is exhausted.

The Bicarbonate Blood Buffer

CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻

This open system is unique: the "weak acid" (H₂CO₃) is in equilibrium with CO₂ gas. The lungs regulate CO₂, and the kidneys regulate HCO₃⁻ — giving the body two physiological "knobs" to control blood pH.

3.4 Laboratory Buffer Preparation: Best Practices

  1. Choose the right system: pKₐ within ±1 of target pH
  2. Decide total concentration: 0.01–0.2 M typical; higher = more capacity
  3. Calculate ratio: [A⁻]/[HA] = 10^(pH–pKₐ)
  4. Prepare stock solutions: Weigh weak acid and conjugate base salt (e.g., NaCH₃COO)
  5. Mix and check pH: Use a calibrated pH meter; adjust with small amounts of strong acid/base if needed
  6. Dilute to final volume: pH should stay nearly constant
  7. Label: Composition, pH, date, preparer

Common Mistakes

  • Using a buffer outside its useful pH range (ratio > 10:1 or < 1:10)
  • Not checking pH after preparation — Henderson–Hasselbalch is approximate
  • Assuming temperature doesn't matter (pKₐ changes with temperature)
  • Contaminating stock solutions (always use clean glassware)
